Productivity & Labor

Output per hour and the pay of the people producing it. The gap between them is real — and its size depends on choices that are rarely shown to the reader. Two of them are live below, on the published BLS series.

This is a descriptive calculation, not evidence of a long-term relationship. It compares two points — the 2017 annual-average baseline and 2026 Q3 — and says nothing about the path between them, about any other period, or about what caused either movement. The gap narrows by — under the sector-matched output deflator and does not close. Values reflect the BLS release current at build time; earlier drafts of this page used a prior vintage (to 2026 Q2) and will not match exactly. It is a worked demonstration that the two deflators answer different questions, and no more than that.

What this chart does not answer

Average, not typical. Compensation here is the average per hour worked, which includes the highest earners. The Economic Policy Institute argues that most of the remaining disagreement in this debate comes down to average versus median pay, and that a typical worker's compensation diverged further. This page does not yet carry a median series, so it cannot test that claim.

Deflator and compensation definition. Martin Feldstein (NBER, 2008) and later James Sherk (Heritage Foundation, 2013) argue that using total compensation and the same output deflator for pay and productivity removes most of the apparent gap. This page applies both of those choices; with the output deflator a gap of about 18–24% remains over the full series, depending on the start date.

Labor share and depreciation. What is left after these adjustments is attributed variously to a falling labor share of income, to faster depreciation of capital (gross versus net output), and to measurement of productivity itself. Each needs its own series; none is loaded here.

The chart is built to show measurement choices, not to settle which side of this debate is right.

Sources, units, definitions, method+
Productivity
Nonfarm Business Sector: Labor Productivity (Output per Hour) for All Workers — series OPHNFB. Index 2017=100, seasonally adjusted, quarterly, Q1 1947 to Q3 2026. U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Productivity and Costs.
Compensation
Nonfarm Business Sector: Real Hourly Compensation for All Workers — series COMPRNFB. Index 2017=100, seasonally adjusted, quarterly, Q1 1947 to Q3 2026. Same release.
Nominal input
Nonfarm Business Sector: Hourly Compensation for All Workers — series COMPNFB. Index 2017=100, seasonally adjusted, quarterly, Q1 1947 to Q3 2026. Both real variants are reconstructed from this one series.
Output deflator
Nonfarm Business Sector: Value-Added Output Price Deflator for All Workers — series IPDNBS. Index 2017=100, seasonally adjusted, quarterly, Q1 1947 to Q3 2026. Sector-matched to the productivity series. GDPDEF is not used: it covers the whole economy and comes from a different agency and release.
Consumer deflator
Constructed by the BLS Office of Productivity and Technology: CPI-U for recent quarters, CPI-U-RS from Q1 1978 through the last complete published calendar year. BLS does not publish it as a series, so it is recovered as COMPNFB ÷ COMPRNFB × 100 — an identity, not an approximation.
Formula
real(t) = COMPNFB(t) ÷ deflator(t) × 100, for both variants. Already-deflated series are never mixed into one chart with self-deflated series. A build-time assertion checks that the recovered deflator reproduces COMPRNFB to floating-point tolerance.
Compensation covers
Wages and benefits per hour worked, including monetary and non-monetary payments to labor.
Related research
BLS, "Understanding the labor productivity and compensation gap" (Beyond the Numbers, Vol. 6 No. 6, June 2017), scope 183 U.S. industries, 1987–2015: under CPI, productivity outpaced compensation in 83% of industries; using the industry output deflator the gap narrowed in 87% of those. Across those industries the median deflator effect and median labor-share effect were similar in magnitude (each around −0.6% per year). This is a reference to test and extend on our own coverage, not a predetermined result of this project, and it does not establish a fixed split for any other industry set or period.
2017 base
The four 2017 quarters of the current vintage average 100.06, not exactly 100: the 2017 = 100 base is set on annual data and later revisions to quarterly values move the average slightly. The two-point box uses the average of the four quarters as its baseline.
Pre-1978 seam
CPI-U-RS begins 1978Q1, so the consumer deflator before that date is a different construction. The splice point is marked on the chart.

Period shown
Restricted to the overlap, March 2006 to September 2026. AHETPI's longer history is not drawn here, because a line that extends past its comparison invites a comparison that does not exist.
Composition effect
These are averages across employees, so the average can move without any individual's pay changing. If employment shifts toward higher-paying industries or occupations — or if lower-paid workers leave payrolls, as in 2020 — the average rises for reasons unrelated to raises. Movements here are not evidence about what happened to a given worker's earnings.
Population mismatch
CES covers private nonfarm establishment payrolls. The productivity measures cover the nonfarm business sector. The two are related but not equivalent, and this chart must not be read as a decomposition of the chart above.

Motion principles

Four rules. Motion encodes change in data or position, never atmosphere alone. One orchestrated moment per chapter, not many small ones. Every animated element has a visible resting state, so the page is complete the moment it loads — the opening line sits under a faint full-length ghost for exactly this reason. Under prefers-reduced-motion, scroll choreography becomes stepped static states and the 3D scenes render as stills, with no content made unreachable.

Mobile

Charts reflow rather than shrink: fewer series, direct labels in place of a legend, annotations collapsed into a stepped sequence. The chapter rail becomes a top progress hairline. Controls become full-width stacked segments with a 44px minimum target. The reader gets the same findings, not a reduced version of them.
